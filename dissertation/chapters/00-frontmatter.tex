\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Much of academic research is necessary work that is not itself scholarship:
finding the relevant literature, turning an idea into an experiment, implementing
it correctly, and assembling the result into a document. That burden falls
unevenly, because those parts are disproportionately \emph{programming} parts ---
a researcher who cannot write code is often unable to run their experiment
without recruiting somebody who can. Language models appear to close the gap, but
two obstacles stand between that offer and a system a university could
responsibly adopt. A plausible review, a plausible experiment and a plausible
result give a non-programming reader no way to tell a correct finding from a
fabricated one; and unpublished work is too sensitive for many institutions to
send to a commercial API.

This dissertation presents a seven-agent research pipeline that addresses both.
It carries a plain-text question through literature retrieval, cross-disciplinary
synthesis, hypothesis generation, experiment planning, code generation and
execution, and a drafting and review cycle, running entirely on the university's
own high-performance computing facility against a self-hosted open-weight model.
Every agent is internally a graph rather than a single prompt, so each model call
and each check is independently inspectable. The organising design rule is that
any decision which can be verified is computed in code rather than accepted from
the model: a hypothesis verdict is derived from what the experiment actually
returned, a citation resolves only against a paper the system genuinely
retrieved, and a verdict is withheld when the inputs were synthesised, when a run
was truncated to fit its compute budget, or when its metrics could not have come
out otherwise.

Those withholding rules are what makes the system's real failures visible, and
that is the second contribution. Across four sweeps of the same thirty-six
research questions and twenty-one runs of a frozen twelve-case benchmark, the
recurring failure is not an experiment that breaks but one that succeeds on the
wrong data. The first sweep published sixteen hypothesis verdicts, nine of them
supporting the hypothesis, computed on datasets that could not answer the
question asked. Closing three verification gates reduced published verdicts to
none --- an honest state, and an unusable one. Staging five real datasets
restored seven, and four of those rest on a dataset the planner substituted for
the one the question required: real data, correctly labelled as real, that simply
does not fit.

No human evaluation was carried out --- a participant cannot be asked to hold an
hour open against a GPU queue that may not clear --- so whether a researcher who
cannot program would be helped by the drafted paper, and whether they would
understand what was withheld from them, remain open. What the work does establish
is narrower and still worth having: that a pipeline of this shape can carry a
question end to end unattended, and that computing verdicts in code rather than
accepting them from a model is what made its failures visible at all.

% ===================== ETHICAL COMPLIANCE =====================
\chapter*{Statement of Ethical Compliance}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Statement of Ethical Compliance}

This project was declared under \textbf{Data Category B} and \textbf{Participant
Category 3} (\textbf{B3}). I confirm that I have read the School's ethical guidance and
followed it throughout the project.

\textbf{Data (Category B).} The pipeline retrieves publicly available academic metadata and
open-access papers from the arXiv, Semantic Scholar and CORE APIs, and uses publicly
available machine learning datasets from Hugging Face in its experimentation stage. All such
data is used in accordance with the respective providers' terms of use. No personal data is
processed by the software itself.

\textbf{Participants (Category 3).} The declaration covered two activities: a requirements
analysis questionnaire completed by academic staff before implementation began, and a
software evaluation of the working system. The questionnaire took place; the evaluation did
not, and no participant was approached and no data collected for it. Participation in the
questionnaire was voluntary and informed; participants consented explicitly before beginning,
could skip any question, and could withdraw at any time. No personally identifiable information was collected,
stored, or reported. All responses are presented here in anonymised and aggregated form, and
the reported detail has been deliberately generalised where a combination of attributes could
otherwise identify an individual within a small institution.
