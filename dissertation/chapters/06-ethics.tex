This project was declared under Data Category~B and Participant Category~3. This chapter sets
out how that declaration was honoured in practice, and the two ethical questions the work
raised that the declaration did not anticipate.

\section{Participants}

The declaration anticipated two activities involving human participants. One took place: the
requirements analysis reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:requirements}, a questionnaire completed by
academic staff before implementation began. It was voluntary, it required explicit consent
before any question could be answered, and participants could skip any item or stop at any
point. No names, email addresses or other identifying fields were collected.

The software evaluation the declaration also covered was not carried out, for the reasons
given in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. No participant was approached for it and no data was
collected under that heading. An activity that is declared and does not happen raises no
ethical difficulty, but it is recorded here so that the declaration and this dissertation
agree.

Anonymisation required more care than the sample size first suggested. With two respondents,
the combination of exact discipline, academic position and institution is close to
identifying within a school of this size, so disciplines are generalised in
Chapter~\ref{ch:requirements} and in the appendices. Free-text responses are reproduced
verbatim because their content is the evidence, but nothing accompanying them narrows the
population they came from.

\section{Data}

The pipeline retrieves publicly available metadata and open-access papers from the arXiv,
Semantic Scholar~\cite{semanticscholar} and CORE~\cite{corapi} APIs, and reads public machine
learning datasets in its experimentation stage, in each case under the provider's own terms.
No personal data is processed by the software. Where a plan's data requirement names a
restricted source --- clinical records under a data use agreement, for instance --- the system
records that the source was not obtained and synthesises a documented surrogate rather than
attempting to acquire it, which is a deliberate design position as much as an ethical one.

\section{Research integrity}

A tool that drafts papers raises an integrity question that a tool which merely analyses data
does not, and two findings during this project sharpened it.

The first is distributive. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2026hallucinations} find not only that
hallucinated citations have risen sharply, but that they disproportionately credit scholars who
are already prominent. A system that fabricates a reference therefore does not merely state a
falsehood; it redirects credit toward those who already hold it, and does so invisibly. This
reframes the citation mechanism described in Section~\ref{sec:writing} as an ethical
safeguard rather than a correctness feature: because a citation marker can only resolve
against a paper the system actually retrieved, the failure mode is structurally unavailable.

The second is the risk of a plausible, well-formatted, and wrong paper. The system is designed
throughout to withhold rather than assert --- a verdict computed on synthesised inputs is
reported as inconclusive, an unresolvable citation is stripped and flagged, an experiment that
did not run is never described as though it had. The incident recorded in
Section~\ref{sec:wrong-data}, in which a feature intended to improve grounding attached an
irrelevant dataset to a question and a positive verdict was asserted on it, is the clearest
demonstration that these safeguards are necessary and that they were not yet complete.

Two limitations follow honestly. The similarity checking promised in the project proposal, and
rated maximally important by both requirements-analysis respondents, was not implemented, so
the system offers no protection against methodological overlap with existing work ---
precisely the failure Gupta and Pruthi~\cite{gupta2025glitters} document. And the tool
produces drafts that a person must take responsibility for; nothing in it establishes
authorship, and it should be understood as assisting a researcher who remains accountable for
what they submit rather than as producing publishable work.

\section{Deployment}

Because the system is intended for institutional use, it runs on the university's own
hardware against self-hosted open-weight models, so that unpublished hypotheses, results and
drafts are never transmitted to a third-party provider (aim~A4). Two deployment weaknesses are
recorded rather than glossed: the web interface has no authentication and binds to the local
loopback interface by default for that reason, and the tunnelling arrangement used for
development exposes an unauthenticated model endpoint for as long as it is open. Both are
acceptable for single-user research use and would need addressing before any shared
deployment.
