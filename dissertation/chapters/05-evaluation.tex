\chapter{Testing and Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

% BUDGET: 7 pages.

Evaluation here operates at three levels: component tests that establish each
deterministic mechanism does what it claims, a frozen benchmark that scores the
code-generation stage repeatedly under controlled conditions, and end-to-end runs
that exercise all seven agents on real research questions. A fourth, the human
evaluation, assesses whether the output is usable by the people the system is
for.

One theme runs through the first three and is stated here because it shaped what
the later sections could claim: on this project, building trustworthy measurement
proved consistently harder than building the thing being measured. Three of the
findings below are defects in the evaluation apparatus rather than in the
pipeline, and in each case the apparatus was reporting the system as worse than
it was.

\section{Component testing}
\label{sec:component-testing}

The suite comprises 1{,}190 tests across 33 files. Its organising principle is
NFR5: all non-model logic must be testable without a language model present.
Table~\ref{tab:deterministic-modules} lists the modules this applies to --- some
4{,}900 lines of logic that runs with no model, no network and no cluster.

\midrule
\texttt{coder/sandbox} & 1862 & environment probes, venv provisioning, execution, static checks \\

Two conventions make this possible. Every environment dependency is injected
through a constructor rather than patched: tests pass
\texttt{network\_check=lambda: False} and a fake dataset lookup instead of
monkeypatching four HTTP endpoints. And the modules above read no settings and
call no model, so they behave identically on a laptop and a compute node.

Tests are written against observed failures wherever one exists. The regression
tests for verdict normalisation use the exact values a benchmark run recorded;
those for the citation checker use the exact reference-list line that produced
thousands of spurious issues. This keeps the suite a record of what actually went
wrong rather than a catalogue of what might.

\section{The Coder benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

The Coder Agent is scored against a frozen corpus of twelve experiment plans
spanning tabular classification, regression, text classification, clustering,
time series, deep learning on a GPU, Monte Carlo simulation, bootstrap
resampling, an infeasible plan that should be refused, and a restricted clinical
source that should be declined. The headline metric is \emph{interpretable}: an
experiment counts only if it ran \emph{and} carries a real boolean verdict.
Completion alone is not enough, because an experiment that finishes on
synthesised data has produced numbers without evidence.

Twenty-one runs were collected on identical inputs, in two series two weeks
apart.

\subsection{The benchmark does not support single-run comparison}
\label{sec:variance}

The five runs under the system's default configuration returned interpretable
scores of \textbf{4, 6, 3, 2 and 3} out of twelve. Completions were far more
stable at 10, 11, 10, 10 and 10, and the count of experiments resolving to real
data varied from 4 to 8.

A threefold spread on identical plans, identical code and the same model means
that a difference of one or two between two runs carries no information. This
matters beyond a caveat, because the benchmark harness ends every run by printing
an invitation to compare it against another single run. The instrument actively
encourages an inference its own variance does not support. Every comparison in
the remainder of this chapter therefore reports the range across repeated runs,
and no claim rests on a single pair.

The variance is not itself a defect. Each stage of the pipeline makes many model
calls, and the corpus is small enough that one experiment changing category moves
the score by 8 percentage points. What was a defect was the absence of repetition
until late in the project: several earlier conclusions about whether a change had
helped were drawn from single runs and could not have been sound.

\subsection{A defect in the measurement, not the system}

One run scored 4 interpretable when the correct figure was 6. Cases 05 and 12 had
each computed a genuine verdict which was recorded as the \emph{string}
\texttt{"True"} or \texttt{"False"} rather than a boolean, and the scorer's strict
identity test rejected both.

The cause is a type crossing a boundary unvalidated. Generated \texttt{evaluate()}
code computes its verdict from a comparison over numpy values, which yields
\texttt{numpy.bool\_}; that is not JSON-serialisable, so the template's
\texttt{json.dump(\ldots, default=str)} stringified it. Downstream,
\texttt{compute\_hypothesis\_verdict} tests \texttt{meets is True}, which a string
fails, so a correct result was silently reported as inconclusive.

It failed safe --- no false claim was ever made, because the strict comparison is
precisely the discipline Chapter~\ref{ch:design} argues for --- but real results
were discarded, and the project's own instrument under-reported its own
improvement. The fix normalises at the origin, where the type is still known,
rather than relaxing the downstream comparison whose strictness is the safety
property. It parses rather than casts, since \texttt{bool("False")} is
\texttt{True} and casting would have converted a refutation into a confirmation.
Results below report both the recorded and the corrected figures.

\subsection{A metric that could not move}
\label{sec:evidence-ready}

Twelve further runs of the same corpus were collected in the final week, with
dataset discovery enabled by default. Every one scored \emph{zero} interpretable
out of twelve, and the score was not wrong. Between one and four experiments per
run found a real dataset, read it and computed their metrics; each had its verdict
withheld because the dataset was found by keyword search and no human had
confirmed that it answers the question (Section~\ref{sec:guardrail-coverage}).

That withholding is correct, and it is also permanent. No improvement to code
generation can open a gate that waits on a person, so a headline metric defined as
``ran \emph{and} carries a verdict'' now reads zero under every configuration of
the agent and will keep reading zero. A run that got four experiments onto real
data and a run that got one are indistinguishable at $0$ versus $0$: the number
had stopped measuring the thing it was introduced to measure.

The scorer was split rather than relaxed. \emph{Interpretable} keeps its strict
meaning; \emph{awaiting source review} counts an experiment that ran on real data
and needs only a human to confirm the source; their sum, \emph{evidence ready}, is
what the agent achieved holding aside a review step that was never the model's
job. The sum is deliberately not named for validity --- an unconfirmed source can
be real data about the wrong subject, which is precisely why the gate exists ---
and the report prints the caution beside the number whenever the two disagree.

Scored that way, and read across all twelve runs --- one agent configuration, one
scorer, two days --- evidence-ready ranges from \textbf{1 to 4} out of twelve with
a median of 3; completions sit at 9 or 10 in every run; total fix attempts range
from 13 to 26. The spread on the headline number is therefore as
wide as the first series' was on a metric defined ten days earlier, while the
count of experiments that merely \emph{finish} barely moves --- which is the more
useful reading of both series. Variance here is a property of the pipeline rather
than an artefact of how success was defined, and it concentrates in exactly the
step that decides whether a run produced evidence.

\section{An ablation: dataset discovery}
\label{sec:ablation}

Section~\ref{sec:wrong-data} shows the dataset discovery mechanism attaching
irrelevant data to experiments. The natural question is whether the pipeline
would be more trustworthy without it. Following the ablation methodology
AutoMETA~\cite{lee2026autometa} applies to its own protocol enforcement, discovery
was disabled and the frozen corpus re-run four times.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{Dataset discovery enabled versus disabled, on identical plans.
Mean with observed range; ranges rather than standard deviations, since four to
five runs cannot support an interval estimate.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{Xcc}
\toprule
& \textbf{Discovery on} ($n{=}5$) & \textbf{Discovery off} ($n{=}4$) \\
\midrule
Completed                  & 10.2 [10--11] & 7.2 [7--8] \\
Interpretable (corrected)  & 3.6 [2--6]    & 0.0 [0--0] \\
Real data only             & 5.0 [4--8]    & 0.2 [0--1] \\
Fix attempts               & 14.6 [12--17] & 36.0 [34--41] \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The interpretable column must not be read as a quality difference. With discovery
off, every resolved input becomes a surrogate, and a surrogate withholds the
verdict by design, so a score of zero is what the provenance mechanism is
\emph{supposed} to produce when no real data exists. Comparing the arms on that
metric is close to tautological.

The informative comparison is throughput, and there the result is unambiguous.
Experiments that never ran at all rose from 2 to 8, and repair effort rose from
14.6 fix attempts to 36.0 --- two and a half times the work for fewer finished
experiments. Offering the model one concrete dataset, with real column names and
real sample rows, is load-bearing for its ability to write working code,
independently of whether that dataset is the right one.

This is the opposite of the expected result, and it settles the design question
the next section raises. Disabling discovery is not the remedy for wrong data: it
costs roughly a third of completions and more than doubles the repair effort while
producing nothing interpretable at all. The remedy has to be stricter matching,
not removal.

\section{Wrong-but-real data}
\label{sec:wrong-data}

The most serious failure this evaluation found is not an experiment that breaks.
It is one that succeeds on the wrong data.

Across both configurations of the frozen corpus, the Hugging Face dataset
\texttt{google/Synthetic-Persona-Chat} --- a corpus of generated dialogue --- was
resolved as the input for \emph{six of the twelve cases}, including clustering,
bootstrap resampling, Monte Carlo simulation and tabular classification. It is
relevant to none of them. The mechanism is visible in the run logs: the search
matched on the literal phrase \emph{``hugging face''} taken from a data
requirement that named no subject. The same behaviour had previously matched a
requirement reading \emph{``public dataset''} to a corpus of public-domain poetry.

The consequence is worse than a surrogate. Every one of those inputs was labelled
\texttt{real\_local} or \texttt{real\_download}, so the provenance mechanism ---
which withholds a verdict only when data was \emph{synthesised} --- had nothing to
object to, and the verdict was asserted. In one case the generated code recorded
in its own \texttt{assumptions\_made} that it had used a web-crawl corpus
\emph{``as a proxy for movie review sentiment corpus''} and derived its labels by
keyword because the corpus had none, and then reported the hypothesis
\emph{supported}.

A surrogate withholds a verdict; wrong real data claims one. This is the
over-claiming direction the entire provenance design exists to prevent, arriving
through a route it did not cover.

Two responses were implemented immediately. The model had \emph{declared} what it
did, in \texttt{assumptions\_made}, and nothing read the declaration; a
deterministic pass now does, withholding the verdict when the text asserts a
substitution while ignoring the conditional fallback the prompt requires.
Separately, an input that was \emph{discovered} rather than named is now marked as
needing confirmation before its verdict stands, on the grounds that a keyword match
is not evidence that a dataset answers a question. Neither closes the case where the
model substitutes silently, which cannot be caught by reading prose.

Both were implemented correctly, and neither was measured until later. The three
subsections that follow report what measuring them found, and they are the most
consequential results in this evaluation: in each case a mechanism that reads
correctly in the source was doing nothing, or the wrong thing, in production.

\subsection{A guardrail with no coverage}
\label{sec:guardrail-coverage}

A confirmation gate is worth only the set of inputs it can see, and asked of a
whole sweep this one had seen none. Across the thirty-six experiments of the second
sweep it fired \emph{zero} times, while all three verdicts that sweep published
rested on datasets found by keyword search. One requirement reading ``Public
benchmark datasets'' resolved to \texttt{gaia-benchmark/results\_public}, a
leaderboard of language-model agent scores, for a hypothesis about gradient-boosted
trees on tabular data; the paper reported it \emph{supported}. A second reported a
refutation off an expected calibration error of exactly $0.0000$ in every arm, and
a third off a Spearman correlation of $1.0$ in both arms.

The cause was a marker written in one place and read in another.
\texttt{needs\_confirmation} tests a \texttt{discovered} field that only the
catalogue connectors ever set, while the Hugging Face lookup --- the path that
resolves most inputs in production --- built its source without it. A keyword hit on
the Hub was therefore indistinguishable from a dataset a human had named. The repair
is a single field. The mechanism's design was never wrong; its coverage was, and
nothing in the test suite could have revealed that, because each half behaved
exactly as its own tests specified.

\subsection{Real data, staged, and still wrong}
\label{sec:staged-hijack}

\texttt{CODER\_DATA\_DIR} is what makes a real verdict reachable at all: data a
human has placed on disk is named rather than found, so its verdict stands. That
property is what made the next defect the most serious one this evaluation found.

Staged files were matched to a requirement by a single shared word of more than
three letters. To help vague requirements find a text corpus, an alias of the 20
Newsgroups corpus had been staged under a filename packing every phrasing the Coder
might use: \texttt{dataset\_benchmark\_standard\_collection\_\ldots\_newsgroups\_%
train.csv}. Under a one-word rule, that filename answers almost any requirement
containing ``dataset'', ``benchmark'' or ``standard''. Twenty-eight of the second
sweep's thirty-six requirements resolved to a staged file, most of them to that one
alias, and across every run recorded in this project nineteen experiments published
a verdict computed on a staged file that could not answer their question --- nine
\emph{supported} and ten \emph{refuted}. A requirement reading ``public EHR dataset
(e.g., MIMIC-III or eICU)'' reached newsgroup posts, because staging is consulted
before the restricted-source rule that exists precisely to refuse that request.

No later gate could catch any of this. A staged input is real and named by
construction, which is the case the entire provenance design treats as trustworthy.

The remedy follows the ablation's conclusion in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}: stricter
matching, not removal. The planner routinely names the dataset it means, in a clause
nothing was reading --- ``public dataset (e.g., UCI Adult dataset or similar tabular
dataset)'', ``public dataset: UCI Electricity Load Diagrams''. Those names are now
extracted and tried first, by the staged-file matcher, the catalogue search and the
Hub lookup alike. A staged file must share two content words with the phrase rather
than one, and vocabulary describing data in general (``benchmark'', ``collection'',
``repository'') is not counted at all.

The threshold was chosen by measurement rather than taste. Over the thirty-eight
distinct requirements that ever reached a staged file, one shared word admitted
twenty-five that could not answer their question while two words admitted none of
them and kept all four correct matches. The majority rule already used by the
catalogue relevance gate was measured too and rejected: it loses the case the
matcher exists for, a requirement naming CMS Medicare claims against a file named
\texttt{medicare\_claims\_2020.csv}.

\subsection{Metrics that could not have come out otherwise}
\label{sec:saturation}

A third route to an unearned verdict needs neither wrong data nor a broken check.
One study in the second sweep was designed --- by the Experiment Planner, not the
Coder --- to split documents at the median entropy and then predict which half a
document fell in, using entropy as a model input. The Coder implemented that plan
faithfully, and accuracy, F1 and $R^2$ all returned exactly $1.0$. The same design
scores $0.99$ on random numbers: the classifier learns a threshold on its own input.

Neither that nor the unit correlation above is a provenance failure or a coding
failure. The measurement had nowhere to go --- a label derived from one of the
model's own inputs, or two arms both pinned at the ceiling, where a difference of
zero is not evidence that one fails to beat the other. Of the 184 completed
experiments recorded in this project, seventeen report every recognisably bounded
metric at exactly its perfect value, and nine of those published a verdict.

A third gate now withholds the verdict in that case, beside the data and compute
gates, and deliberately does not route back to the fix loop. The defect is usually a
sound implementation of an unsound plan, and regenerating code does not touch the
plan: one run spent seven consecutive attempts failing the existing hollow-metrics
check without ever escaping it.

\section{End-to-end runs}
\label{sec:end-to-end}

Thirty-six interdisciplinary research questions (sweep~1 of the three compared in
Section~\ref{sec:three-sweeps}) were run through the complete
pipeline, spanning finance, health, climate, economics, language and the physical
sciences. All thirty-six completed, producing 108 drafted papers across three
writer/reviewer iterations each, with a mean of 3.9 cross-field bridge insights
per run. An earlier sweep of twenty questions had completed three; the difference
is attributable to question design --- each of the thirty-six names a concrete
data type and an explicit baseline --- and to hardware that left a GPU free for
the experiments themselves.

At the Coder stage, 25 of 36 experiments completed and 11 generated code that was
not run. Of the completed ones, 9 reported a hypothesis supported, 7 refuted, and
9 had their verdict withheld.

\subsection{A verification mechanism that failed by over-reporting}

Every one of the 36 runs exhausted its three writer/reviewer iterations without
passing review, and mean issue counts \emph{rose} across iterations, from 34.8 to
36.0 to 36.8. Revision improved 17 runs, worsened 17 and left 2 unchanged.

The cause was a false-positive storm in the deterministic citation check.
Table~\ref{tab:citation-issues} gives the breakdown.

The checker was scanning the paper's own reference list. Entries render as
\emph{``Riccardo Alberti and Sven Karbach (2026). Title\ldots''}, so the
narrative-citation pattern captured the \emph{last} author's surname before the
year, while the lookup is keyed on the \emph{first}. Every multi-author reference
therefore failed to resolve and was reported as possibly fabricated. A single
paper produced 55 spurious issues from its reference list against 44
citation-shaped matches in its entire body.

Two conclusions follow. The Writer's citation grounding was working essentially
perfectly: four genuine citation issues across 108 drafts. And the failure mode of
a verification mechanism matters as much as its coverage --- by over-reporting,
this check made \texttt{overall\_pass} unreachable, rendered the revision loop
inert, and made a guarantee that \emph{was} holding impossible to confirm. A
checker that cries wolf is not merely noisy; it destroys the signal it exists to
provide. Reference entries are rendered mechanically from the retrieved paper
index and so cannot be fabricated by construction; they are now excluded.

\section{Four sweeps of the same questions}
\label{sec:three-sweeps}

The thirty-six questions were run four times over eleven days: once before the
mechanisms of Section~\ref{sec:wrong-data} existed (sweep~1, the sweep reported
above), once after the three verification checks were repaired (sweep~2), once
after the three data gates of Sections~\ref{sec:guardrail-coverage}
to~\ref{sec:saturation} were closed (sweep~3), and once after five real datasets
were staged on the cluster and an inventory of them was placed in front of the
Experiment Planner (sweep~4). The questions, the model and the cluster are
identical throughout, so the columns of Table~\ref{tab:three-sweeps} differ only
in which guardrails were in force and what data was within reach.

Sweeps~1 to~3 are the guardrails closing, and the third column is their cost.
Published verdicts fall from sixteen to three to none, and each fall removes claims
the run had not earned: sweep~1's sixteen include nine \emph{supported} verdicts
computed on staged files that could not answer their question, and sweep~2's three
are the leaderboard, the perfect-calibration and the unit-correlation results
described above. By sweep~3 nothing is over-claimed, because nothing is claimed.

That is the honest state of the system at that point and it should not be dressed
up. Twenty-six experiments ran to completion in sweep~3 and every one had its
verdict withheld: sixteen for synthetic inputs, ten for real data found by keyword
search that no human has confirmed. What stands between this pipeline and a usable
result is therefore not reliability --- it completes more experiments than it did at
the start, with fewer failures --- but \emph{confirmation}. A verdict requires data
somebody has vouched for, and the only two mechanisms that produce such data today
are a human staging a file and a human approving a discovered one.

The name extraction works, and works best where it was aimed. Where a plan names a
dataset in an example clause the search now asks for that dataset: ``(e.g., German
Credit Dataset\ldots)'' fetched \texttt{AiresPucrs/german-credit-data} on three
separate questions, ``(e.g., XNLI\ldots)'' fetched \texttt{facebook/xnli}, and
``(e.g., arXiv CS category\ldots)'' fetched an arXiv category corpus --- against a
baseline in which ``public dataset'' had fetched a web-crawl corpus and a
text-to-SQL corpus. The failure that remains is visible in the same trace and is the
obvious next increment: a plan naming a \emph{repository} rather than a dataset
(``public dataset repositories (e.g., UCI ML Repository)'') is taken literally, and
the search returned the Iris dataset for three unrelated questions, one of them on
probability calibration for imbalanced financial data. Those verdicts were withheld
--- the system behaving correctly on data it should never have fetched.

\subsection{Staging restores the verdict}
\label{sec:staging-restores}

Sweep~4 changed no guardrail. It changed what was on the shelf. Five real datasets
--- UCI Credit Approval, Statlog German Credit, Adult Census Income, an hourly
air-quality series and the MoleculeNet ESOL solubility set --- were staged on
cluster scratch with a README recording each one's source, columns and units, and
the inventory was named to the Experiment Planner rather than left for a keyword
search to stumble on.

It is the largest single-change effect in this evaluation. Plans now name a file
outright (\texttt{plan source: uci\_statlog\_german\_credit.csv}); seventeen of the
twenty resolved inputs matched a staged file, \emph{none} was found by search, and
seven verdicts were published --- six refutations and one confirmation --- against
zero the sweep before. Four plans were additionally refused outright by the
Experiment Planner as infeasible, the first time that path fired in any sweep, each
naming data the staged set does not hold: time-to-event data with censoring,
industrial sensor data with known degradation times, multi-station weather records,
and a tokenizer adapted to a domain.

What that demonstrates is narrower than it first appears, and more useful. It is not
that the agent became better at finding data; nothing about its searching changed.
It is that the binding constraint on this pipeline stating a result is the
availability of data somebody has vouched for, and that an afternoon of staging
lifts the constraint for whichever questions the staged files happen to fit.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} takes that as the deployment finding it is: a department
that stages its own corpora gets verdicts, and one that expects the agent to go and
find them does not.

\subsection{The Planner substitutes what is on the shelf}
\label{sec:planner-substitution}

The cost arrives one stage upstream of every gate that exists. A staged file is
real, and it is named, so \texttt{provenance.py} has nothing to object to; what
nothing checks is whether it can answer the question that was asked. Four of the
seven published verdicts rest on a dataset that cannot.

Question~15 asks whether spatial autocorrelation improves prediction. The plan
selected the single-site air-quality series, which carries no coordinates, so the
generated code invented them --- recording in its own assumptions that the dataset
``does not contain actual latitude and longitude coordinates, so synthetic ones are
generated'' and that these are ``sufficient to demonstrate the spatial lag
concept''. A refutation was published off invented geography. Question~6 asks about
feature drift over time and was given German Credit, which has no time dimension;
the code cut it into ``10 chronological time slices of 100 samples each'', and that
is the sweep's single \emph{supported} verdict. Question~33 requires ``a tabular
dataset with inherent time ordering'' and assumes German Credit's
\texttt{duration\_months} and \texttt{age\_years} supply it; neither is a
chronology. Question~3 asks about fraud detection and ran on credit \emph{approval}
data, while its own assumptions note that the chosen metric suits ``fraud detection
scenarios''.

The other three are sound, and one is a genuine result: question~29 found that
active selection needed 29 simulations against random sampling's 22 --- a 31.8\%
\emph{increase} where the hypothesis predicted a 30\% reduction --- refuting it on a
dataset that does answer the question.

This is the wrong-but-real failure of Section~\ref{sec:wrong-data} arriving by its
third distinct route, and the route was opened by the repair that closed the second.
Discovered data is withheld pending confirmation; synthesised data is withheld
outright; staged data is real and named and clears both, so whether it \emph{fits}
is never asked. No provenance check can ask it, because every provenance check is a
question about where data came from. Fitness is a question about the plan, and the
plan is where it will have to be asked.

\subsection{What the record cannot say}
\label{sec:read-not-recorded}

Thirteen of the twenty resolved inputs were downgraded to surrogate because the
generated code declared a substitution in \texttt{assumptions\_made} and the
deterministic pass of Section~\ref{sec:wrong-data} read the declaration. Ten of
those thirteen name a staged file that exists, at exactly the absolute path the
code passes to \texttt{read\_csv}.

Two say so outright: ``the UCI Statlog German Credit dataset was not found at the
expected path, so a synthetic dataset was created''. The file is there, with the
thousand rows and twenty-one columns the code expects. Whether the fallback branch
actually executed cannot be settled from what the run recorded, because nothing
records it: a completed experiment keeps its \texttt{run.py}, its
\texttt{results.json} and its two provenance documents, and no trace of which branch
of the guarded read ran. The indirect evidence favours the fallback having fired ---
every accuracy in that experiment sits flat at the 70\% base rate across all seven
training-set sizes, which is what labels drawn independently of features produce,
while another experiment reading the same staged file reports 0.77 --- but the point
stands either way: a verdict is being withheld on the strength of a sentence the
model wrote before its program ran.

The discriminator is, in the end, grammatical mood. The same sweep contains
question~33, whose assumptions include ``the synthetic dataset generation provides a
reasonable approximation when the real dataset is unavailable'' --- a conditional
describing the fallback the prompt requires, correctly ignored --- and its verdict
was published on data its own baseline accuracy confirms was real. An assertion
withholds and a conditional does not, and nothing checks which branch the program
took. The remedy is small and is not a model problem: the template already hands
generated code a \texttt{log\_progress} call, and one marker emitted from each side
of the guarded read would replace an inference about prose with a fact about
execution. It is recorded here rather than implemented because a repair that cannot
be measured before submission is a claim, not a result.

\subsection{Two failures that never reached the model}

Nineteen experiments ran in sweep~4 against twenty-six in sweep~3. Of the twelve
that did not, eight exhausted the fix budget on a traceback and one on metrics that
failed the plausibility check, which is the loop working as designed. Two never
reached the model's code at all. One asked for \texttt{gensim} in an environment
\texttt{uv} had resolved to Python~3.14.7, for which no wheel is published, and the
fallback source build failed compiling a C extension. The other requested
\texttt{skopt} and \texttt{sklearn} --- the \emph{import} names of
\texttt{scikit-optimize} and \texttt{scikit-learn}, both of which the same
requirements list also names correctly --- and resolution failed as unsatisfiable,
because no distribution called \texttt{skopt} exists.

Both are deterministic defects in the layer beneath the agent: an interpreter floor
nobody set, and a missing import-name-to-distribution mapping. Neither is visible in
any metric this chapter reports except as a lower completion count, which is exactly
how a systemic cause disguises itself as model weakness --- the same confusion the
failure classification of Chapter~\ref{ch:design} exists to prevent, one layer
further down than it currently looks.

The writer/reviewer loop is unmoved by all of this, as it should be: every mechanism
above is data-side. No paper passed review in any of the four sweeps. What changed
is the composition of what remains, and it is stark. In sweep~4 the three
deterministic checks account for 392 findings against 7{,}257 from the model-judged
hallucination check --- 95\% of the total, the same proportion as sweep~3 --- and in
sweep~2, where the locations were tabulated, 86\% of those fell in a single section,
Related Work. Section~\ref{sec:end-to-end} showed one deterministic check failing by
over-reporting. What the three later sweeps show is that repairing it exposes a
second, much larger over-reporting problem underneath it, in the one check no
deterministic rule can replace.

\section{What this evaluation does not establish}
\label{sec:limitations}

A human evaluation was planned, and both requirements-analysis respondents had
agreed in principle to take part. It was not carried out, for a reason that is
itself a finding about deploying this kind of system on shared research
infrastructure.

An evaluation session requires a working system in front of a participant at an
agreed time. The pipeline needs a GPU to serve its model, and the cluster it runs on
allocates GPUs by queue rather than on demand: a job waits behind other users, the
per-user GPU limit is shared with anything else the account is already running, and
the partitions with capacity actually available are pre-emptible --- two benchmark
runs in this project were stopped mid-run by pre-emption, and the final sweep was
restricted to one question at a time because half the account's GPU allowance was in
use elsewhere. A participant cannot be asked to hold an hour open against a queue
that may not clear, and a session that begins and is then pre-empted is worse than
none. Compounding this, the guardrail failures of
Sections~\ref{sec:guardrail-coverage} to~\ref{sec:saturation} were found late, and
repairing them was judged a better use of the remaining time than measuring a system
whose verdicts were at that point demonstrably unsound.

Section~\ref{sec:future-overlap} sets out how the evaluation should be run, including
the two ways the scheduling obstacle can be removed. Its consequences are stated here
rather than absorbed quietly, because it bounds several claims.

Nothing in this chapter establishes that the output is \emph{usable}. Every result
above concerns what the system does --- what it completes, what it verifies, what it
refuses --- and none of it is evidence about whether a researcher who cannot program
would be helped by the drafted paper, would understand what had been withheld from
them, or would calibrate their trust correctly. Those are the questions A1 and A3
raise, and the questions Chapter~\ref{ch:requirements}'s respondents raised first.
They remain open, and they are the largest gap in this dissertation's evidence.

Four further limits apply to what \emph{is} reported. Each sweep is a single run of
its configuration. Thirty-six questions is a larger sample than the twelve-case
benchmark whose variance Section~\ref{sec:variance} measures, and the differences in
Table~\ref{tab:three-sweeps} are mostly far larger than that instrument's spread ---
but a difference of one or two in any row of that table should be read as noise, and
the direction of a change is better evidence there than its size. Every run used a
single model on a single cluster, so nothing here separates a property of the pipeline from a
property of Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct. The thirty-six questions were written for
this evaluation by one person and each names a concrete data type and an explicit
baseline, which makes them a favourable sample rather than a representative one. And
there is no comparison against a human baseline or a competing system: the
comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:three-sweeps} are internal, measuring the effect of
specific repairs on a fixed set of questions, and say nothing about how the pipeline
compares with a researcher doing the same work unaided.

Two repairs this chapter argues for were not made. The substitution of staged data
for the data a question requires (Section~\ref{sec:planner-substitution}) is
described and not fixed, so four of sweep~4's seven published verdicts should be
read as demonstrations of the failure rather than as results. The runtime marker
that would let provenance record which branch of a guarded read executed
(Section~\ref{sec:read-not-recorded}) is likewise specified and not implemented. In
both cases a repair landing after the last sweep would have been an unmeasured
claim, and the project has already recorded what unmeasured claims cost it.

What the evidence does support is narrower, and still worth having: that a pipeline
of this shape can carry a question end to end unattended; that its characteristic
failures are identifiable, reproducible and in several cases repairable; and that
computing verdicts in code rather than accepting them from a model is what made
those failures visible at all.

\section{Assessment against the aims}
\label{sec:assessment}

\begin{description}[style=unboxed,leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item[\textbf{A1}] \emph{Question to draft paper without writing code.} Met
    mechanically, and unevaluated for usefulness. Thirty-four of the last sweep's
    thirty-six questions produced a drafted paper with no human intervention, and
    across three sweeps that figure never fell below thirty-three. Whether such a
    paper helps the researcher it is written for was not put to anyone
    (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
  \item[\textbf{A2}] \emph{No unverifiable claim.} Met as of sweep~3, and only
    after three separate failures of the same kind. Citations cannot resolve to
    unretrieved papers, verdicts derive from execution, and synthesised inputs
    withhold conclusions --- but real-but-wrong data asserted verdicts through a
    confirmation gate with no coverage (Section~\ref{sec:guardrail-coverage}),
    through a staged-file match on one shared word
    (Section~\ref{sec:staged-hijack}), and through measurements that could not have
    come out otherwise (Section~\ref{sec:saturation}). Sweep~3 published no verdict
    at all, which satisfies this aim in the only way it can currently be satisfied
    --- and sweep~4 shows what satisfying it that way was hiding. Once real data was
    staged, verdicts returned, and four of the seven rest on a staged dataset the
    Planner substituted for the one the question needed
    (Section~\ref{sec:planner-substitution}). That is the same failure a fourth
    time, through the one route no provenance check can cover. The aim is met for
    every claim about where data came from and unmet for whether that data fits;
    a silent substitution the model never declares also remains uncaught.
  \item[\textbf{A3}] \emph{Independently usable and steerable stages.} Met in the
    implementation, untested with users. Each agent validates its predecessor's
    contract and runs standalone, from the command line or from a previous run's
    JSON, and the interface exposes stage ranges, user-supplied papers and
    hypothesis selection (D5). That the mechanism exists is demonstrated; that it is
    the steering a researcher wants is not.
  \item[\textbf{A4}] \emph{Institutional infrastructure, open weights.} Met: every
    run reported here executed on the university's cluster against a self-hosted
    model, with no research content leaving the institution.
  \item[\textbf{A5}] \emph{Experiments recover from their own failures.} Met and
    quantified: 284 fix attempts across the runs collected, with the repair
    categories and their resolution rates reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:po}.
  \item[\textbf{A6}] \emph{Survive shared-HPC conditions.} Met: runs resume from
    durable checkpoints, batch sweeps resume per question from a manifest, and
    twenty-one benchmark runs and four 36-question sweeps completed on contended,
    pre-emptible partitions under a per-user GPU quota. Pre-emption stopped two
    benchmark runs mid-corpus and the final sweep ran one question at a time for
    two days; both finished, which is the property being claimed.
\end{description}
